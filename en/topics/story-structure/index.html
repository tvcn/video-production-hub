TOPIC / story

Structure and boards

Opening, scene, turn, pace and storyboards: once the audience knows whom to follow, the camera knows what to look at.

20 guides · grouped by topic, not publish date

  1. Story / Screenplay

    The One-Line Story: Who Wants What, and What Stands in the Way

    Compress a vague idea into a shootable sentence: a specific person, a specific want, and a specific obstacle.

  2. Story / Storyboard

    The Shot Sheet: Turn One Line into Eight Shootable Shots

    It can look rough. The crew just needs to read it. One shot sheet answers three things: who, where, and what happens next.

  3. Story / Structure

    Three Acts: Put the Character Against a Wall

    Act one gives a goal, act two applies pressure, act three forces a choice. Each act does one job.

  4. Story / Interview

    Stop Asking “How Did You Feel”: Ask for an Action

    People rarely remember a feeling. They remember which foot they moved first. Specific questions get human answers.

  5. Story / Dialogue

    Dialogue: Let Them Fight with Words, Not Explain

    Dialogue stalls when the writer says everything the character is thinking. Good lines fight for something, the listener reacts, and the backstory stays on screen.

  6. Story / Conflict

    Conflict: Both Sides Need a Point

    An argument is not conflict. Real conflict is two valid wants that cannot both win. The audience has to understand both sides before it can hurt.

  7. Story / Character

    Character Arc: Not Better, Different Choices

    An arc is not a character turning good. Under the same pressure, they first choose safety and finally choose the cost. The audience sees the choice change, not a new personality label.

  8. Story / Voice-Over

    Voice-Over: Do Not Summarize What the Picture Already Says

    Voice-over is not the picture read aloud. It either gives information the picture cannot, or gives one person's judgment. If you can shoot it, do not say it. If you must say it, keep it short, concrete, and biased.

  9. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    Want and Stakes: What Does Losing Cost?

    What a character wants matters less than what losing it costs.

  10. Story & Screenwriting / Screenplay

    Scene Description: Only What the Camera Can See

    Describe only what the camera can see: light, action, objects, sound. Leave inner life to the actor.

  11. Story & Screenwriting / Storyboard

    Shot Sheet: Number, Framing, Action, Duration

    Four columns are enough: number, framing, action, duration. If these are vague, the set will be chaos.

  12. Story & Screenwriting / Directing

    Rehearsal: Block First, Lines Later

    Block first, scripts in hand. Once the body is in the right place, the lines have somewhere to land.

  13. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    Escalation: Same Wall, Higher Cost

    Hit the same obstacle three times and raise the cost each time; only then does the character look cornered.

  14. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    Midpoint Turn: Change the Method, Not the Goal

    The midpoint is not a plot twist; it is the character changing methods to pursue the same want.

  15. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    A Sequence: Three Scenes, One Change

    One scene delivers one change; three scenes together move a sequence.

  16. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    Subplot: Only the One That Presses the Main Line

    A subplot is not extra noise; it must squeeze the main line at one specific point.

  17. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    Theme Through Action, Not Speeches

    Do not let a character speak the theme; make them choose under pressure and reveal it.

  18. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    Ending: Stop After the Choice

    End after the choice. The sentence the audience supplies is stronger than a closing explanation.

  19. Story & Screenwriting / Story

    Short-Film Economy: Cut What You Cannot Shoot

    Cut what you cannot shoot, then make what remains more specific.

  20. Story & Screenwriting / Screenplay

    Script Format: Make the Whole Crew Read It the Same Way

    Script format is not for a literary prize; it helps camera, sound, and edit find information at a glance.
